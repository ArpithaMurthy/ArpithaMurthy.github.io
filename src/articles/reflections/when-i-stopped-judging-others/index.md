# When I Stopped Judging Others

I used to think that judging others was mostly about them. I did not realize how much it was shaping the way I saw myself. When I stopped measuring other people's choices and success, I became less harsh with myself. I may have benefited more than anyone else from learning not to judge.

## My own harshest critic

For a long time, I was my own critic and sometimes my own enemy. I often felt that I was not enough or not good at something. Even when nobody else was criticizing me, I could find reasons to criticize myself.

Part of this came from comparison. I noticed what other people had achieved, how quickly they seemed to progress, and how confidently they appeared to move through life. I measured them, and then I used the same scale against myself.

Judgment had quietly become a habit. Once my mind learned to rank people, it did not leave me out of the ranking.

## The stories I imagine others tell about me

Sociologist Charles Horton Cooley called this the **looking-glass self**: our view of ourselves can be shaped partly by how we imagine other people see us. I can imagine how I appear to someone, guess what they think of me, and then respond to that imagined judgment as though it were a fact. Cooley developed this idea in *Human Nature and the Social Order* (1902).

That idea stayed with me. Sometimes I was not reacting to what another person had actually said or thought. I was reacting to my own guess about their judgment. I could feel inadequate in front of an opinion that might never have existed.

## Becoming softer toward others and myself

When I stopped judging others so quickly, something unexpected happened. I also became softer toward myself.

I began to remember that I rarely know another person's full story: what they have faced, what support they received, what they fear, or what effort their life requires. Their success does not prove that I have failed, and their mistakes do not make me better than them.

The same kindness applies inward. I also have a story that cannot be captured by one result, one weakness, or one comparison. I can recognize where I need to grow without treating myself as unworthy.

## Would I still choose this if no one were watching?

I have started asking myself another question: if I were the only person in the world, would I still make this choice? Would I still want this career, this possession, this achievement, or this appearance if there were nobody to impress and nobody with whom to compare myself?

The question does not mean that other people are unimportant. I still need relationships, community, and shared purpose. But it helps me separate what I genuinely value from what I want only because it may change how others rank me.

I can spend a lot of energy tracking temporary rankings and wondering where I stand. Asking whether I would still choose something if nobody were watching helps me notice what I value for its own sake: what I learn, what I create, and how I care for people.

## What changed for me

Not judging does not mean that every action is right or that I should abandon discernment. I can disagree, set boundaries, and learn from what I observe. The difference is that I do not need to reduce a whole person, including myself, to a verdict.

I am still learning this. Judgment can return before I even notice it. But now I try to pause and ask whether I understand enough to decide what another person deserves, and whether I would speak to myself as harshly as the voice in my mind does.

I thought giving up judgment would mainly make me kinder to others. Instead, it also softened some of my own criticism. I do not have to be better than someone else to be enough; I can focus on living by my values and treating both myself and others with more patience.
