# A Computer on Your Desk: Operating Systems and Desktop Apps

For decades, computing meant going to the computer—or sending work to the people who operated it. Personal computers changed the direction of that relationship. The machine came to the user, and software became something people could buy, install, learn, and use for a task at home or work.

## From hobbyist kit to household tool

The Altair 8800, announced in 1975, arrived as a kit that appealed to hobbyists willing to assemble and program a computer. A few years later, machines such as the Apple II and TRS-80 arrived as more complete products. They could be connected to a television, used to learn programming, and adapted for tasks that had previously required specialized equipment.

Early personal computers often opened to a command prompt. Users typed commands and learned the machine's vocabulary. Applications such as spreadsheets showed why that effort could be worthwhile: a person could explore a budget, change assumptions, and immediately see the result. Software was not only for calculating what an organization already knew; it could become a tool for thinking.

## The operating system becomes the front door

As computers gained more programs and users, the operating system became the layer that coordinated hardware and made applications possible. It managed files, memory, devices, and the running of programs. On early personal computers, that experience was often command-driven.

A graphical user interface offered a different invitation: windows, icons, menus, and a pointer. Research at Xerox PARC influenced later commercial systems; Apple's Macintosh brought a successful mouse-driven interface to a broad consumer audience in 1984. The operating system was no longer just an invisible manager. It shaped how a person understood the computer.

## A desktop becomes a place

Word processors, drawing programs, games, and spreadsheets gave the computer recognizable jobs. Files and folders made digital work feel organized like a desk. Developers could create products for a growing installed base, while operating-system vendors controlled important rules about hardware support and application distribution.

This arrangement also created friction: people installed and updated software on individual machines, compatibility varied, and work often stayed on the computer where it was created. Those limitations would make a networked alternative increasingly attractive.

## What changed for people?

The computer became a personal workspace. People no longer needed to ask a central operator to run every task; they could choose an application and act directly. That shift helped turn software from a specialist's tool into a product for millions of users.
